Executive Overview 
This report presents a consolidated analysis of the Student Analytics Profiles (SAPs) for the 25 
students enrolled in Data Analytics for AI Business, Group 9226, Academic Year 2026 , at the 
Graduate School of Business (GSB), Miami, AASTMT. The analysis is intended to transform the 
information collected through the SAP from descriptive student -profile data into a structured basis for 
course planning, differentiated learning, analytics -readiness assessment, balanced team 
formation, and the development of professionally relevant group projects . Accordingly, the report 
examines students’ professional backgrounds, business contexts, decision responsibilities, data 
environments, Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), analytics proficiency, Power BI readiness, AI and 
Machine Learning exposure, and learning expectations. 
The cohort is characterized by substantial professional and sectoral diversity . Students represent 
government and digital transformation, SAP and enterprise architecture, construction and infrastructure, 
quality assurance and quality control, mining and metallurgy, railway systems, heavy -equipment 
maintenance, biomedical engineering, healthcare and pharmaceuticals, oil and gas, marine operations, 
finance and investment, credit and accounts receivable, software and SaaS, product management, and 
diversified business operations. A significant proportion of the cohort consists of experienced 
professionals occupying managerial, technical -leadership, consulting, entrepreneurial, or executive 
roles. Consequently, the instructional challenge is not primarily to introduce students to business 
problems; rather, it is to enable them to convert their existing  domain expertise and managerial 
experience into structured, data-driven and increasingly AI-enabled decision-making capability. 
Despite the diversity of industries represented, the SAP analysis identifies considerable commonality in 
the students’ analytical requirements. Their business problems frequently involve forecasting and 
prediction, financial performance and cost control, predictive maintenance, asset reliability, 
quality improvement, process optimization, customer and market analysis, resource allocation, 
project performance, service delivery, and digital transformation . Similarly, the KPIs reported across 
the cohort converge around several common performance domains, including revenue growth, 
profitability, EBITDA, ROI, budget variance, cash flow, equipment availability, MTBF, MTTR, downtime, 
failure rate, utilization, throughput, NCR closure, rework, SLA compliance, turnaround time, customer 
conversion, forecast accuracy, digital adoption, system availability, safety compliance, and project 
delivery performance. This convergence provides an important pedagogical opportunity: students may 
work in very different professional environments while learning and applying a  common business 
analytics methodology. 
A particularly important finding is the prevalence of fragmented organizational data environments . 
Students frequently describe information distributed across SAP and other ERP systems, Excel 
spreadsheets, Aconex, Jira, maintenance -management systems, operational applications, financial 
systems, project platforms, and manually generated reports. The principal development requirement is 
therefore not simply learning individual analytics tools. Students need to understand how to move from 
fragmented operational data toward an integrated analytical process in which data are prepared,

validated, modelled, transformed into meaningful KPIs, analyzed, visualized, interpreted, and ultimately 
used to support managerial decisions. 
The resulting learning needs can be organized into nine interconnected areas: data preparation and data 
quality; data integration and modelling; Power BI and visualization; KPI design and performance 
analytics; descriptive and diagnostic analytics; predictive analytics; prescriptive analytics; AI and 
Machine Learning for business; and data storytelling, governance and responsible AI use . These 
findings support a course structure that begins with the managerial problem rather than the analytical 
technique. The recommended learning sequence is therefore: Business Challenge → Business Problem 
→ Decision Question → Data Requirements → Data Preparation and Integration → KPI Framework → 
Descriptive Analytics → Diagnostic Analytics → Predictive Analytics → Prescriptive Analytics → Power 
BI and AI -Enabled Decision Support → Managerial Action . Such a sequence is particularly appropriate 
for an MBA course because analytics is positioned as a means of improving decisions rather than as an 
isolated technical exercise. 
To provide a quantitative basis for assessing student readiness, the report introduces the Data Analytics 
Readiness Score Index (DARSI) . DARSI is a diagnostic 0 –100 index derived from three dimensions 
represented in the SAP: Analytics Proficiency (40%), Power BI/Business Intelligence Readiness (35%), 
and AI/Machine Learning Exposure (25%) . Qualitative self-assessment responses are normalized onto a 
common numerical scale, allowing the cohort to be examined using four broad readiness bands: 
Foundation (0 –39), Developing/Transitional (40 –54), Intermediate/Analytics -Ready (55 –74), and 
Advanced/Applied (75 –100). Where sufficient SAP evidence is unavailable, the student remains 
unclassified rather than being assigned an inferred score. DARSI is therefore intended strictly as a 
learning-readiness diagnostic indicator and not as an academic grade or judgment of student 
performance. 
At cohort level, the quantitative analysis indicates a mean Analytics Proficiency score of approximately 
60.2/100, Power BI/BI Readiness of 42.4/100, AI/ML Exposure of 54.6/100, and an overall DARSI of 
approximately 52.6/100  among students for whom usable readiness information is available. The 
pattern is significant. The cohort's general analytical and managerial understanding is stronger than its 
practical BI capability, making Power BI and applied data modelling the clearest common technical 
development area . AI exposure is already meaningful but uneven: some students report introductory 
familiarity, while others have practical experience with GenAI, automation, Power Platform, advanced 
dashboards, DAX, AI agents, computer vision, or other AI -enabled applications. This variation confirms 
the need for differentiated practical learning rather than assuming that all students should begin at the 
same technical level. 
The readiness grouping is appropriately treated as an unsupervised-learning problem , because the 
analysis initially seeks to identify similarities among students rather than predict a previously validated 
readiness label. For a cohort of 25 students, hierarchical clustering or K -medoids provides an appropriate 
basis for exploring readiness patterns, while a future expanded SAP containing mixed numerical, ordinal 
and categorical variables could be analyzed using Gower distance combined with hierarchical 
clustering. As additional cohorts and post -course outcomes become available, the analytical framework

could subsequently evolve toward supervised learning, using classification to predict validated readiness 
categories and regression to estimate post-course readiness or learning gain. 
The readiness analysis also provides a direct basis for differentiated learning. Foundation-level students 
require greater emphasis on data understanding, cleaning, descriptive statistics, KPI development and 
Power BI fundamentals. Developing students can progress toward data modelling, diagnostic analytics, 
visualization and introductory forecasting. Intermediate students can undertake more substantial 
predictive analytics, classification, advanced Power BI and AI -assisted analysis. Advanced students can 
be challenged through more sophisticated DAX, predictive modelling, optimization, automation, Machine 
Learning and AI integration. Importantly, these differentiated technical pathways should operate within a  
common conceptual framework , ensuring that every student understands the complete relationship 
between business problems, data, analytics and managerial decisions. 
For group projects, the analysis recommends forming five teams of five students , with team 
composition based on a combination of analytics readiness, industry diversity, functional background 
and managerial experience. The objective is to avoid concentrating technically advanced students in a 
small number of teams while simultaneously ensuring that each team contains sufficient analytical 
capability to undertake a meaningful MBA -level project. Each team should therefore combine stronger 
analytics members with intermediate, developing and foundation -level participants wherever feasible. 
This structure is intended to support  peer learning, cross -functional collaboration and knowledge 
transfer, while maintaining individual responsibility for analytical work. 
Five integrated project themes have been identified to reflect the professional backgrounds of the 
proposed teams: AI-Enabled Enterprise Performance and Financial Decision Support; Predictive 
Quality and Operational Performance Analytics for Asset -Intensive Projects; AI -Driven Predictive 
Maintenance and Asset Reliability; AI -Assisted Resource Allocation and Business Performance 
Optimization; and AI -Enabled Operational Intelligence and Digital Transformation . Although the 
projects address different sectors and business problems, they should follow the same academic 
architecture: defining the business challenge and decision question, identifying and preparing the 
dataset, establishing KPIs, conducting descriptive and diagnostic analysis, applying appropriate 
predictive and prescriptive techniques, developing a Power BI dashboard, incorporating a meaningful 
AI/ML component, and translating the analytical results into managerial recommendations. This common 
architecture provides comparability across teams while preserving direct relevance to students’ 
professional environments. 
Where organizational confidentiality permits, teams should be encouraged to use real but appropriately 
anonymized professional data . Where access to real data is restricted , particularly in government, 
finance, healthcare, railway, corporate or other sensitive environments , students may construct 
synthetic datasets that reproduce the structure and business logic of the actual problem without 
disclosing confidential information. Similarly, because the course focuses on Data Analytics for AI 
Business, the use of AI should be substantive rather than cosmetic. AI may support data preparation, 
forecasting, classification, anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, scenario analysis, model

interpretation, RAG-based information retrieval, or AI -assisted decision support; it should not be limited 
to generating the written project report. 
Overall, the SAP analysis indicates that Group 9226 should be approached as a cohort of experienced 
professionals developing the capability to transform domain expertise and organizational data into 
evidence-based and AI -enabled managerial decisions . The principal developmental gap lies not in the 
students' knowledge of their industries, but in systematically connecting business challenges with data 
preparation, KPI design, analytics, visualization, predictive modelling, AI and managerial interpretation. 
The course should therefore emphasize the complete decision -support cycle rather than the isolated 
mastery of individual software applications. 
In this context, the Student Analytics Profile can evolve from an intake questionnaire into a broader 
learning analytics and course -design instrument  supporting four interconnected functions: pre-
course diagnosis, differentiated learning design, evidence -based team and project formation, and 
post-course learning -gain measurement . The DARSI model provides an initial quantitative foundation 
for this approach and can subsequently be applied before and after the course to calculate changes in 
analytics readiness. The overarching educational framework emerging from the analysis can therefore be 
summarized as: 
Domain Expertise + Business Problem + Data + KPIs + Analytics + AI → Better Managerial Decisions  
 
This framework provides a coherent foundation for aligning the professional diversity of Group 9226 with 
the MBA orientation of the course and its central objective: developing practical Data Analytics for AI 
Business capability that produces measurable managerial value.

1.5 Dominant Cohort Analytics Challenges 
• Data fragmentation across SAP, ERP, Excel, Aconex, Jira, maintenance systems, operational 
platforms and manual reports. 
• Movement from static reporting toward interactive BI, diagnostic analytics, prediction and decision 
support. 
• Prediction needs: equipment failure, stockouts, payment delays, demand, cost overruns, service 
delays and resource requirements. 
• Optimization needs: capital, workforce, maintenance, credit, visits, equipment utilization and 
investment prioritization. 
• Need to integrate business expertise with data preparation, KPI definition, visualization, modelling 
and managerial interpretation.

Conclusion 
The consolidated analysis of the Student Analytics Profiles (SAPs) for Group 9226 – Data Analytics for 
AI Business demonstrates that the cohort should be approached as a group of experienced professionals 
seeking to strengthen their ability to transform professional knowledge and organizational data into 
evidence-based and AI -enabled managerial decisions . The students represent diverse sectors —
including government, digital transformation, construction, infrastructure, mining, railway systems, 
healthcare, pharmaceuticals, finance, oil and gas, marine operations, enterprise systems, software and 
entrepreneurship—yet their business challenges reveal considerable analytical commonality. 
Forecasting, cost and performance control, predictive maintenance, quality improvement, resource 
allocation, customer analysis, process optimization and digital transformation recur across the cohort, 
providing a strong basis for a common applied analytics framework. 
The analysis also indicates that the principal developmental gap is not a lack of professional or 
managerial knowledge. Students generally understand their industries, operational processes, 
organizational challenges and relevant performance indicators. The more significant challenge is 
converting this knowledge into a systematic analytical process involving data preparation, integration, 
KPI design, visualization, diagnostic analysis, prediction, optimization and managerial 
interpretation. Cohort-level readiness results reinforce this conclusion: general analytics understanding 
is comparatively stronger than practical Business Intelligence capability, while AI and Machine Learning 
exposure is meaningful but uneven. Accordingly, Power BI, data modelling and preparation, KPI analytics, 
predictive methods and the effective integration of AI into decision support should form central 
components of the practical learning experience. 
The proposed Data Analytics Readiness Score Index (DARSI)  provides a quantitative foundation for 
recognizing these differences in student readiness. By integrating Analytics Proficiency, Power BI/BI 
Readiness and AI/ML Exposure, DARSI can support differentiated learning and evidence -based team 
formation. It should, however, remain a diagnostic developmental indicator rather than an academic 
grade. Students with incomplete readiness information should remain unclassified until sufficient 
evidence becomes available. In future cohorts, applying DARSI at both the beginning and end of the 
course would also permit the calculation of learning gain through ΔDARSI = DARSI Post − DARSI Pre , 
providing an additional measure of course impact. 
The proposed formation of five balanced project teams  translates the readiness analysis into practical 
course design. Teams combine different levels of analytics readiness with complementary professional 
and functional backgrounds, supporting peer learning while avoiding the concentration of technically 
stronger students within a small number of groups. The five proposed project themes —enterprise 
performance and financial decision intelligence, predictive quality and infrastructure performance, 
predictive maintenance and asset reliability, strategic resource allocation and business optimization, and 
digital transformation and operational intelligence —are aligned with students' actual professional 
environments while maintaining common academic requirements. 
All projects should therefore follow a consistent analytical pathway: Business Challenge → Business 
Problem → Decision Question → Data → KPIs → Descriptive Analytics → Diagnostic Analytics →

Predictive Analytics → Prescriptive Analytics → Power BI → AI/ML → Managerial Recommendations . 
Real anonymized professional data should be encouraged wherever appropriate; where confidentiality or 
organizational restrictions apply, synthetic datasets should reproduce the structure and decision logic of 
the real problem without disclosing sensitive information. Similarly, AI should contribute to a genuine 
analytical task —such as forecasting, classification, anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, 
optimization, model interpretation or decision support —rather than being used merely to generate 
project text. 
More broadly, the SAP has the potential to evolve from an intake questionnaire into a learning analytics 
and course -design instrument  supporting four interconnected functions: pre-course diagnosis, 
differentiated learning, evidence -based team and project formation, and post -course learning-gain 
measurement. This would establish a stronger link between student characteristics, instructional 
design, applied project work and measurable development of analytics capability. 
The overall conclusion is therefore that the course should not be centred primarily on teaching analytics 
software. Its central purpose should be to develop the students' ability to frame managerial problems 
analytically, identify and prepare appropriate data, construct meaningful KPIs, select appropriate 
analytical and AI methods, interpret results, and translate evidence into defensible managerial 
action. The professional expertise already exists within Group 9226; the role of the course is to build the 
analytical bridge between that expertise and better decision-making. 
The resulting educational framework can be summarized as: 
Domain Expertise + Business Problem + Data + KPIs + Analytics + AI → Better Managerial Decisions  
 
This framework provides a coherent foundation for aligning the professional diversity of Group 9226 with 
the MBA orientation of Data Analytics for AI Business , while maintaining a clear focus on the ultimate 
purpose of analytics and AI: creating measurable value through better managerial decisions.
